\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Gandal and Shy (2001) study how national recognition of foreign product standards interacts with conversion costs and network effects. Their framework combines segmented national markets with spatial product differentiation and government choices over whether foreign standards are recognized. In the conversion-cost case, unrestricted mutual recognition is the benchmark outcome, but two countries may prefer to form a standardization union that recognizes each member's standard while excluding the third country's standard. When conversion costs are sufficiently high, the excluded firm's sales in member countries fall to zero. Their Proposition 3 then compares the welfare of a union member with its welfare under mutual recognition \citep{gandalshy2001}.

This note revisits only the price subgame after that foreclosure occurs in the published quadratic-transport specification. The foreclosure threshold itself is correct. With two union firms facing zero conversion cost and the outsider facing cost $c$, the outsider's three-firm interior share is $1-2c/5$ and reaches zero at $c=5/2$. Once the outsider is absent from realized sales, however, the two surviving firms divide both the short arc of length one and the long arc of length two that connect their locations. Under squared-distance transportation costs, the long-arc marginal consumer responds differently to a price difference than the short-arc marginal consumer. Re-solving that boundary changes the two-firm residual-demand slope relative to the calculation used in the published Appendix B.

The first result is a corrected post-foreclosure equilibrium. For $5/2<c<3$, the member firms charge $p_M=c-1$. The price is below the unconstrained two-firm price because a higher member price would permit the outsider to regain a positive neighborhood of demand; the exclusion constraint therefore binds and the member firms engage in limit pricing. For $3\leq c<5$, conversion costs are high enough that the outsider remains foreclosed even when the member firms charge the unconstrained symmetric duopoly price, $p_M=2$. The published quadratic model thus contains an intermediate regime between three-firm competition and unconstrained duopoly. The constant post-foreclosure member price $3/2$ is recovered only at the boundary $c=5/2$.

The second result concerns welfare. Correcting the equilibrium prices does not reverse the member-country comparison in Proposition 3. Under the corrected two-firm allocation, consumer surplus in a union member is $3V-3p_M-3/4$, while that country's firm earns $3p_M+1$ from the two union markets and the recognizing outsider market. The price terms cancel, leaving member-country surplus $3V+1/4$, exactly one half above the mutual-recognition level $3V-1/4$. The corrected price therefore changes consumer payments, producer profits, and the strategic mechanism supporting foreclosure, but not the headline member-country welfare ranking.

The scope of the note is deliberately narrow. It does not challenge the threshold at which foreclosure starts, does not claim novelty for limit pricing as a general mechanism, and does not overturn Proposition 3. The contribution is to provide the Nash equilibrium implied by the quadratic transportation technology in the published model, identify the binding-exclusion regime that this equilibrium creates, and establish directly why the original welfare ranking remains valid under the corrected prices. Section \ref{sec:calculation} isolates the demand calculation, Section \ref{sec:equilibrium} characterizes the corrected equilibrium, and Section \ref{sec:welfare} recomputes member-country welfare. A short appendix records boundary details and secondary consistency corrections without enlarging the two-result structure of the note.
