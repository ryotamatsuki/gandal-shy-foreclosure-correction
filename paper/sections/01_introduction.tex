\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Product standards shape international market access not only through the technical requirements imposed on goods but also through the rules determining which national standards are recognized across borders. Harmonization and mutual-recognition arrangements can lower barriers among participating countries while changing the competitive position of suppliers from outside the agreement. These effects are central to the economics of regional standards arrangements and technical barriers to trade \citep{gandalshy2001,chenmattoo2008,costinot2008,klimenko2009}. Related work also emphasizes that standards can alter the relative position of domestic and foreign producers and need not operate as simple tariff equivalents \citep{marettebeghin2010,bertifalvey2018}. Within this literature, a basic strategic question is easy to state but economically distinct from the usual market-access question: if a standards arrangement drives a foreign firm's equilibrium sales to zero, does that firm cease to constrain the prices charged by the remaining suppliers?

Gandal and Shy (2001) provide a particularly clean setting in which to answer that question. In their conversion-cost model, two countries may form a standardization union that recognizes each member's standard while excluding the third country's standard. Firms are located symmetrically on a Salop circle, consumers incur quadratic transportation disutility, and a firm selling under an unrecognized standard must incur a conversion cost. In a union member market, the two member firms face zero conversion cost while the outsider faces cost $c$. The published three-firm interior equilibrium implies outsider demand $q_O=1-2c/5$, so the outsider is foreclosed when $c$ reaches $5/2$. Proposition 3 then compares the welfare of a union member with its welfare under mutual recognition \citep{gandalshy2001}.

This note leaves that model and the foreclosure threshold unchanged and revisits only the price subgame after foreclosure in the published quadratic-transport specification. Once the outsider has no realized sales, the surviving member firms compete across both the short arc of length one and the long arc of length two joining their locations. Under squared-distance transportation costs, the location of the marginal consumer on the long arc responds differently to a price difference than on the short arc. Re-solving that boundary changes residual demand relative to the calculation in Appendix B of Gandal and Shy (2001). The issue is therefore not a change in primitives or an alternative equilibrium concept. It is an incentive constraint within the published model: the excluded firm can remain relevant because a sufficiently high member price restores positive outsider demand.

The corrected equilibrium has two post-foreclosure regimes. For $5/2<c<3$, member firms charge $p_M=c-1$. At that price the outsider has zero demand, but a higher member price would make entry into positive sales profitable at the outsider's marginal conversion cost. The foreign firm therefore imposes a binding competitive constraint even though it makes no equilibrium sales. In this interval the member firms engage in limit pricing. For $3\leq c<5$, conversion costs are sufficiently high that the outsider remains foreclosed at the unconstrained symmetric duopoly price, $p_M=2$. The model thus exhibits a three-stage competitive pattern as conversion costs rise: three active firms, foreclosure with a binding outsider constraint, and finally unconstrained two-firm pricing. The constant post-foreclosure price $3/2$ reported in the published calculation is obtained only at the boundary $c=5/2$.

This distinction matters even though the headline welfare ranking survives. Correcting the price equilibrium changes consumer expenditure and member-firm profits throughout the foreclosure region, so the original welfare comparison cannot simply be carried over without recomputation. Under the corrected two-firm allocation, however, the relevant price terms offset in national welfare: a union member's consumer surplus is $3V-3p_M-3/4$, while its domestic firm earns $3p_M+1$ across the two union markets and the recognizing nonmember market. National surplus is therefore $3V+1/4$, one half above the mutual-recognition level used in Proposition 3. The result provides a robustness check on the original policy comparison: the equilibrium characterization changes over a nondegenerate parameter interval, but the member-country welfare ranking remains valid for a structural reason rather than because the corrected prices happen to be close to the published ones.

The contribution is deliberately narrow. First, the note gives the price equilibrium implied by the quadratic transportation technology in Gandal and Shy (2001) and identifies the omitted binding-foreclosure region. Second, it clarifies the economic mechanism: foreclosure of realized foreign sales need not imply the disappearance of foreign competitive discipline. Third, it re-establishes the original member-country welfare ranking under the corrected equilibrium. The note does not claim novelty for limit pricing as a general mechanism, does not alter the foreclosure threshold, and does not propose a broader model of contemporary standard-setting. Its purpose is to make the strategic and welfare implications of this international-standards benchmark internally consistent.
