\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Foreclosure is an allocation outcome, but it need not imply that an excluded rival has become strategically irrelevant. In a pricing game, a firm that sells nothing in equilibrium can still constrain active firms if a small increase in their prices would restore profitable demand for the excluded firm. The distinction between realized exclusion and the competitive constraint imposed by a rival is therefore potentially important for equilibrium pricing. Foreclosure and exclusion are established themes in industrial organization \citep{reytir2007}. The standardization-union model of \citet{gandalshy2001} provides a particularly transparent setting in which to examine this distinction because an outsider can lose all sales in member markets when its conversion cost becomes sufficiently high.

This note revisits only the post-foreclosure price subgame in the published quadratic-transport specification of Gandal and Shy (2001). Their foreclosure threshold is correct. With two member firms facing zero conversion cost and the outsider facing cost $c$, the outsider's three-firm interior share is $1-2c/5$ and reaches zero at $c=5/2$. Once realized outsider sales fall to zero, however, the two surviving firms compete across both the short and long arcs joining their locations. Under squared-distance transportation costs, the long-arc marginal consumer responds differently to a price difference than the short-arc marginal consumer. Re-solving that boundary changes post-foreclosure residual demand relative to the calculation in Appendix B. The issue is therefore not a change in primitives or in the foreclosure threshold, but an equilibrium incentive constraint that remains relevant after the outsider's equilibrium quantity has become zero.

The corrected equilibrium has two post-foreclosure regimes. For $5/2<c<3$, member firms charge $p_M=c-1$. A higher price would restore outsider demand, so the exclusion constraint binds and the zero-sales outsider continues to discipline member pricing. For $3\leq c<5$, conversion costs are high enough that the outsider remains excluded at the unconstrained symmetric duopoly price, $p_M=2$. The model thus moves from three-firm competition to a binding-exclusion regime and then to unconstrained duopoly. We refer to the intermediate regime as limit pricing because the members price below their unconstrained duopoly optimum at the highest symmetric price consistent with continued exclusion. Limit pricing itself is, of course, a longstanding industrial-organization concept; the mechanism here is not the informational signaling studied in classic models such as \citet{milgromroberts1982}, but a binding product-market exclusion constraint in the published standardization model.

Correcting the price equilibrium does not reverse the member-country welfare comparison in Proposition 3 of Gandal and Shy (2001). Under the corrected two-firm allocation, consumer surplus in a union member is $3V-3p_M-3/4$, while its domestic firm earns $3p_M+1$ across the two union markets and the recognizing outsider market. The price terms cancel, leaving national surplus $3V+1/4$, one half above the mutual-recognition level considered in the original proposition. The corrected pricing equilibrium therefore changes strategic behavior and the distribution of surplus while leaving this particular national-welfare ranking intact.

The contribution is deliberately narrow. The note corrects the post-foreclosure Nash equilibrium implied by the published quadratic transportation technology, identifies the omitted nondegenerate limit-pricing region, and shows why the original welfare ranking is robust to that correction. It does not claim novelty for foreclosure or limit pricing as general mechanisms, alter the model's primitives, or extend the analysis to new institutional environments. The application remains international product standards and compatibility, a literature that subsequently developed along different institutional and market-structure dimensions \citep{costinot2008,klimenko2009}; the contribution here is the industrial-organization characterization of pricing incentives inside the original benchmark.
