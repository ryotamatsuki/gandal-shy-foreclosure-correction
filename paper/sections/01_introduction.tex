\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

International product standards and recognition rules affect the cost of serving foreign markets and can determine whether a foreign producer remains an effective competitor. \citet{gandalshy2001} study these issues in a three-country model in which countries can recognize foreign standards or form a standardization union. Within a union, member firms sell without conversion costs while an outsider faces a conversion cost in member markets. The model provides a compact benchmark for comparing standards institutions in international trade; related work has examined alternative institutional arrangements and technical-compatibility agreements \citep{costinot2008,klimenko2009}. This note revisits one element of that benchmark: the price subgame after the outsider is foreclosed in a union member market.

The foreclosure threshold itself is not in dispute. In the published quadratic-transport specification, with two member firms facing zero conversion cost and the outsider facing cost $c$, the outsider's three-firm interior share is $1-2c/5$ and reaches zero at $c=5/2$. Once realized outsider sales fall to zero, however, the two surviving firms compete across both the short and long arcs joining their locations. Under squared-distance transportation costs, the long-arc marginal consumer responds differently to a price difference than the short-arc marginal consumer. Re-solving that boundary changes post-foreclosure residual demand relative to the calculation in Appendix B. The issue is therefore not a change in the standardization policy, the model's primitives, or the foreclosure threshold, but the price equilibrium that implements the foreclosed outcome.

The corrected equilibrium has two post-foreclosure regimes. For $5/2<c<3$, member firms charge $p_M=c-1$. A higher member price would restore outsider demand, so the exclusion constraint binds even though the outsider sells nothing in equilibrium. For $3\leq c<5$, conversion costs are high enough that the outsider remains excluded at the unconstrained symmetric duopoly price, $p_M=2$. The benchmark therefore moves from three-firm competition to a binding-exclusion regime and then to unconstrained duopoly. We refer to the intermediate regime as limit pricing because the members choose the highest symmetric price consistent with continued exclusion, below their unconstrained duopoly optimum. This is a product-market exclusion mechanism, not the informational signaling mechanism of classic limit-pricing models such as \citet{milgromroberts1982}. More generally, foreclosure and exclusion need not imply that an excluded rival has become strategically irrelevant \citep{reytir2007}.

The pricing correction does not reverse the member-country welfare comparison in Proposition 3 of \citet{gandalshy2001}. Under the corrected two-firm allocation, consumer surplus in a union member is $3V-3p_M-3/4$, while its domestic firm earns $3p_M+1$ across the two union markets and the recognizing outsider market. The price terms cancel, leaving national surplus $3V+1/4$, one half above the mutual-recognition level considered in the original proposition. The corrected equilibrium therefore changes member prices, consumer expenditure, producer profit, and the strategic path through which standardization excludes the outsider, while leaving this particular national-welfare ranking intact.

The contribution is deliberately narrow. The note corrects the post-foreclosure Nash equilibrium in the published standardization-union benchmark, identifies the omitted nondegenerate limit-pricing region, and re-establishes the original welfare ranking under the corrected equilibrium. It does not claim novelty for foreclosure or limit pricing as general mechanisms, alter the underlying standards institutions, or add a new trade-policy environment. For the international-economics application, the key distinction is between realized exclusion and the competitive constraint created by potential re-entry: a standardization union can eliminate outsider sales while the outsider still disciplines member pricing. The welfare result then provides a separate robustness check on the policy comparison rather than a reason to treat the equilibrium correction as immaterial.
