\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Gandal and Shy (2001) study how national recognition of foreign product standards interacts with conversion costs and network effects. In their conversion-cost model, two countries may form a standardization union that recognizes each member's standard while excluding the third country's standard. When conversion costs are sufficiently high, the excluded firm's sales in member countries fall to zero, and Proposition 3 compares a member country's welfare with its welfare under mutual recognition \citep{gandalshy2001}. International agreements over product standards and technical compatibility have remained part of the subsequent trade-policy literature, although later papers analyze different institutional and market structures \citep{costinot2008,klimenko2009}.

This note revisits only the price subgame after foreclosure in the published quadratic-transport specification. The foreclosure threshold is correct: with two member firms facing zero conversion cost and the outsider facing cost $c$, the outsider's three-firm interior share is $1-2c/5$ and reaches zero at $c=5/2$. Once the outsider has no realized sales, however, the surviving firms compete across both the short arc of length one and the long arc of length two joining their locations. Under squared-distance transportation costs, the long-arc marginal consumer reacts less to a price difference than the short-arc marginal consumer. Re-solving that boundary changes post-foreclosure residual demand relative to the calculation in Appendix B.

The corrected equilibrium has two regimes. For $5/2<c<3$, member firms charge $p_M=c-1$. A higher price would restore outsider demand, so the foreclosure constraint binds and the outsider disciplines prices despite making no sales. For $3\leq c<5$, conversion costs are high enough that the outsider remains foreclosed at the unconstrained symmetric duopoly price, $p_M=2$. The published quadratic model therefore contains an intermediate limit-pricing regime between three-firm competition and unconstrained duopoly. The constant post-foreclosure price $3/2$ is recovered only at the boundary $c=5/2$.

Correcting prices does not reverse the member-country comparison in Proposition 3. Under the corrected two-firm allocation, consumer surplus in a union member is $3V-3p_M-3/4$, while its domestic firm earns $3p_M+1$ across the two union markets and the recognizing outsider market. The price terms cancel, leaving national surplus $3V+1/4$, one half above the mutual-recognition level. The note therefore makes a narrow claim: it corrects the Nash equilibrium implied by the published quadratic transportation technology, identifies the resulting binding-foreclosure regime, and establishes why the original member-country welfare ranking remains valid. It does not challenge the foreclosure threshold, claim novelty for limit pricing as a general mechanism, or overturn Proposition 3.
