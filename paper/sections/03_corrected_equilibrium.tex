\section{Corrected post-foreclosure equilibrium}\label{sec:equilibrium}

We now solve the price subgame implied by \eqref{eq:duodemand}. If the outsider remains foreclosed, firm 1's profit in a member market is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:duoprofit}
 \pi_1^{F}(p_1;p_2)=p_1\left[\frac32+\frac34(p_2-p_1)\right].
\end{equation}
This expression is strictly concave in $p_1$. Its first-order condition is
\[
 \frac32+\frac34p_2-\frac32p_1=0,
 \qquad\text{or}\qquad
 p_1=1+\frac{p_2}{2}.
\]
Thus, if the two member firms could ignore the possibility that a higher price restores outsider demand, their symmetric equilibrium price would be
\begin{equation}\label{eq:unconstrained-duopoly}
 p_1=p_2=2.
\end{equation}
The key issue is whether this unconstrained two-firm price is compatible with continued foreclosure.

At the outsider's own location, the shortest distance to either member firm is one. If both member firms charge $p_M$, a consumer located there obtains net utility $V-p_M-1$ from a member product. The outsider's lowest sustainable price is its marginal conversion cost $c$, which gives utility $V-c$ at its own location. The outsider therefore has no positive neighborhood of demand only if the member firms' delivered price is no greater than $c$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:foreclosure-constraint}
 p_M+1\leq c,
 \qquad\text{or equivalently}\qquad
 p_M\leq c-1.
\end{equation}
Equality makes the consumer at the outsider's ideal point indifferent, a measure-zero event. The constraint is slack at the unconstrained duopoly price $2$ if and only if $c\geq3$. This creates a second threshold inside the foreclosure region.

\begin{proposition}[Corrected post-foreclosure equilibrium]\label{prop:equilibrium}
In a standardization-union member market of the quadratic-transport model, suppose $5/2<c<5$ so that the outsider is foreclosed. The symmetric equilibrium allocation satisfies
\[
 q_1^*=q_2^*=\frac32,\qquad q_3^*=0,
\]
and the member-firm price is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:piecewise-price}
 p_M^*(c)=
 \begin{cases}
 c-1, & \dfrac52<c<3,\\[4pt]
 2, & 3\leq c<5.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
For $5/2<c<3$, the foreclosure constraint binds and the member firms engage in limit pricing. For $3\leq c<5$, the constraint is slack and the member price equals the unconstrained symmetric duopoly price.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Fix the other member price at the symmetric candidate and consider unilateral deviations by firm 1. First take $5/2<c<3$. Because $c-1<2$, the unconstrained price in \eqref{eq:unconstrained-duopoly} violates \eqref{eq:foreclosure-constraint}. A symmetric foreclosing equilibrium, if it exists, must therefore lie at or below $c-1$.

For prices that maintain foreclosure, profit is \eqref{eq:duoprofit}. If $p_2=c-1$, the unconstrained maximizer of that concave function is
\[
 1+\frac{p_2}{2}=\frac{c+1}{2}.
\]
For $c<3$, this exceeds $c-1$. Hence firm 1's foreclosed-regime profit is increasing throughout the feasible interval up to the boundary $p_1=c-1$. Equivalently, the derivative at the boundary from the foreclosure side is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:left-derivative}
 \left.\frac{\partial\pi_1}{\partial p_1}\right|_{-}
 =\frac32-\frac34(c-1)
 =\frac34(3-c)>0.
\end{equation}
No downward deviation can therefore improve on the boundary price.

Now consider an upward deviation. Once $p_1$ exceeds $c-1$, firm 3 regains demand near its ideal point. Holding $p_2=c-1$ and $p_3=c$, firm 1's local three-firm demand is
\[
 q_1=1+\frac{p_2+p_3-2p_1}{2}
 =c+\frac12-p_1.
\]
The associated profit $p_1(c+1/2-p_1)$ is strictly concave and has unconstrained maximizer $(c+1/2)/2$. For $c>5/2$, that maximizer lies strictly below $c-1$. Thus profit is already decreasing on the entry side when the boundary is crossed. The one-sided derivative is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:right-derivative}
 \left.\frac{\partial\pi_1}{\partial p_1}\right|_{+}
 =\frac32-(c-1)
 =\frac52-c<0.
\end{equation}
The price $p_1=c-1$ is therefore a global kink optimum across the two relevant demand regimes: cutting price sacrifices margin while preserving an already foreclosed outsider, whereas raising price admits the outsider and reduces demand. By symmetry, $p_1=p_2=c-1$ is the member-price equilibrium for $5/2<c<3$.

For $c\geq3$, the unconstrained two-firm price $2$ satisfies $2\leq c-1$, so the outsider remains foreclosed at the ordinary symmetric duopoly optimum. Because \eqref{eq:duoprofit} is strictly concave, no price change that preserves foreclosure is profitable. A deviation large enough to admit the outsider cannot help either: at any given deviating price, the entrant can only weakly reduce firm 1's demand relative to the two-firm demand used in \eqref{eq:duoprofit}, whose maximum already occurs at $p_1=2$. Hence $p_M=2$ is a best response for each member firm.

Finally, symmetry gives $q_1=q_2=3/2$ whenever the outsider has zero demand. At $c=3$, the two price branches meet because $c-1=2$. At the lower boundary $c=5/2$, the limit-pricing branch converges to $p_M=3/2$, exactly the limiting member price from the three-active-firm solution in \eqref{eq:interior-su}. This completes the characterization.
\end{proof}

Proposition \ref{prop:equilibrium} changes the post-foreclosure regime structure without changing the onset of foreclosure. As $c$ increases, the published quadratic model passes through three economically distinct phases. For $c<5/2$, all three firms are active and the outsider's demand falls continuously with $c$. At $c=5/2$ its share reaches zero. For $5/2<c<3$, the outsider remains a competitive constraint even though it makes no sales: a member firm that raises its price past $c-1$ makes entry locally profitable. The union firms therefore price at the exclusion boundary rather than at their unconstrained duopoly best response. Only once $c$ reaches three does the excluded firm cease to constrain member pricing, after which the standard two-firm price $2$ obtains.

This distinction matters for the economic interpretation of the model. Foreclosure of realized sales does not immediately imply that the excluded firm is irrelevant for equilibrium conduct. Over the interval $5/2<c<3$, its potential re-entry disciplines the two active firms. The correction therefore inserts a limit-pricing phase between three-firm competition and ordinary duopoly rather than simply replacing one constant post-foreclosure price with another.

A direct deviation illustrates the Nash failure of the published price away from the boundary, although it is not needed for the proof. At $c=4$, the profile $p_1=p_2=3/2$ gives firm 1 quantity $3/2$ and profit $9/4$. Raising only $p_1$ to $7/4$ leaves the outsider foreclosed; \eqref{eq:duodemand} then gives quantity $21/16$, so profit rises to $147/64$, a gain of $3/64$.\footnote{For $c\geq3$, prices of a zero-demand outsider need not be unique. Proposition \ref{prop:equilibrium} therefore characterizes the economically relevant member prices and allocation rather than asserting uniqueness of an arbitrary zero-sales outsider price.}
